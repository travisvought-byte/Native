Finding your Nation

If you grew up away from your Nation, or you are not sure which Nation your family comes from, this page explains how to trace your family and how enrollment works.

Enrollment is a legal status that each Nation decides for itself. Finding community is a separate thing, and you can start on both at once: see Finding community.

What enrollment is

Each tribe sets its own rules for who can be a member, and only the tribe can enroll you. The Department of the Interior says two rules are common: descending directly from someone named on the tribe's base roll, or being related to a member who does. Some tribes also look at blood quantum, where you live, or whether your family has stayed in contact with the tribe.

The Bureau of Indian Affairs (BIA) does not keep a list of enrolled members and does not do family research for people. Each tribe keeps its own records. The BIA also says the rules for federal services differ from program to program, so being a descendant, being enrolled and being eligible for a given program are three different questions. The labels on our help listings say which one each program asks about.

Sources: Department of the Interior, Tribal Enrollment Process · BIA, Tracing American Indian and Alaska Native Ancestry · BIA, Frequently Asked Questions. Checked November 17, 2026.

Tracing your family, step by step

  1. Write down what your family knows. Names, birth dates and birthplaces of parents, grandparents and great-grandparents, and any papers kept at home. Note who told you which Nation the family comes from.
  2. Work out which Nation your ancestor belonged to. You will be dealing with that tribe's government, so this is the question everything else depends on.
  3. Build the paper trail. The BIA says you need to show your link to an ancestor with records the tribe accepts. Birth and death certificates come from the vital records office of the state where the person was born or died. The BIA also points to the National Archives (federal census records, including the Bureau's own censuses, the Dawes Rolls and military records), county courthouses, and town, school and church records, newspapers, libraries and historical societies.
  4. Contact the tribe's enrollment office. Ask for its membership rules and the papers it accepts. Our Nations directory has a phone number and website for each government office.
  5. Apply to the tribe if you meet its rules. The tribe decides.

Source: BIA, Tracing American Indian and Alaska Native Ancestry. Checked November 17, 2026.

About DNA tests

The BIA says blood and DNA tests cannot show that you descend from a particular tribe. A test may show that you are related to a tribal member, and that only helps if the tribe accepts such tests. Ask the tribe before paying for one.

The CDIB card

A Certificate of Degree of Indian Blood (CDIB) is issued by the BIA. To get one you must show a direct line, through a parent, grandparent or great-grandparent, to an ancestor who is listed with a degree of Indian blood on official tribal rolls. Aunts, uncles and cousins cannot be used. The links are shown with birth and death certificates. Applications are handled by enrollment staff at BIA regional and agency offices.

Sources: BIA ancestry guide · BIA Division of Tribal Government Services. Checked November 17, 2026.

If you were adopted, in foster care, or your family was separated

  • The National Indian Child Welfare Association hears every year from people tracing their Native ancestry and has guides for families.
  • If a child welfare case is open now, read about the Indian Child Welfare Act. The child's tribe has a right to be told and to take part.
  • If your relatives were sent to an Indian boarding school, the National Indian Boarding School Digital Archive holds digitized school records and student name lists.
  • In Michigan, state Native American Outreach Services workers help families identify their heritage and tribal enrollment, including people who are not sure of their tribal affiliation.

If your family's story starts in Ohio

No federally recognized Nation has its government in Ohio today, but dozens of Nations are tied to Ohio by homelands and treaties, and their governments are in other states now. The Ohio page lists the Nations the Ohio History Connection names, with a phone number for each.

If your Nation is not federally recognized

Our directory comes from the federal list, so Nations recognized only by a state, and Nations still seeking recognition, are not in it yet. Some help does not depend on federal recognition: the American Indian College Fund, for example, accepts members and descendants of state-recognized tribes. On the help page you can show only the listings that do not ask for tribal enrollment.
